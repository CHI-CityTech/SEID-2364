## 1. Reflection on My Ethical Development
When I entered this course, I thought of ethics as a set of rules: avoid harm, respect privacy, be fair. My understanding was shallow and mostly procedural. Over the semester, my thinking shifted from “ethics as compliance” to “ethics as a practice of responsibility within complex systems.” The early assignments forced me to confront how harm emerges not only from malicious intent but from structural design choices, institutional incentives, and the way information moves through sociotechnical systems. The BBS framework, in particular, changed how I see technology: not as a tool acting on the world, but as a mediator that transforms signals, authority, and agency.

The biggest shift in my thinking came from the mediation‑pathways work. Before this course, I assumed that if an AI system produced a harmful outcome, the problem was either the model or the data. Now I understand that harm is often produced in the spaces between agents — in the transitions, handoffs, and interpretations. The Robert Williams case crystallized this for me: the facial recognition system didn’t arrest him, but it reshaped the entire investigative workflow in a way that made the arrest predictable. That insight fundamentally changed how I think about responsibility.

Another major development was recognizing that ethical positions are not static. They evolve as systems evolve. Big Idea 5 — the need for future‑resilient ethical positions — pushed me to think beyond current technologies and consider how my values hold up under new pressures. I realized that my earlier ethical assumptions were too dependent on present‑day contexts. I now see ethics as something that must be adaptable, principled, and grounded in a clear hierarchy of values.

Overall, this course moved me from a narrow, individualistic view of ethics to a systemic, relational, and forward‑looking one. I now understand that ethical practice requires not only good intentions but also structural awareness, critical reflection, and a willingness to revise my assumptions as technologies and institutions change.

## 2. Personal Ethical Position Statement
### 2A. Personal Ethical Position (One Paragraph)
I prioritize justice, transparency, and human dignity as the core of my ethical stance. When these values conflict, I place justice first — meaning I favor outcomes that reduce structural inequity, even when doing so requires slowing down, challenging norms, or rejecting efficiency. Transparency comes next, because people deserve to understand systems that affect their lives. Human dignity anchors both: no system should treat people as data points or expendable errors. When situations become ethically complex, I default to protecting those with the least power, because they bear the greatest risk when systems fail.

### 2B. Analysis of Influences & Foundations
My position is shaped by the course’s emphasis on mediation, harm pathways, and distributed responsibility. The BBS framework taught me that ethical problems are rarely isolated; they emerge from interactions across physical, virtual, and institutional spaces. This pushed me toward a justice‑centered stance, because inequity often emerges in the transitions between agents, not in the intentions of any single actor.

Philosophically, my position aligns most closely with Rawlsian justice and care ethics. Rawls influences my prioritization of fairness and protection of the least advantaged. Care ethics influences my focus on relational responsibility and the lived experiences of those affected by systems. I diverge from strict consequentialism because I believe some harms — especially those involving dignity — cannot be justified by aggregate benefits.

This position also reflects my discomfort with opacity in sociotechnical systems. Transparency is not just a procedural value; it is a moral one. People deserve to know how decisions about them are made, especially when those decisions carry risk. Ultimately, my personal ethical stance is grounded in the belief that systems must be designed and governed with attention to power, vulnerability, and the lived realities of those most affected.

## 3. Professional Ethical Position Statement
###3A. Professional Ethical Position (One Paragraph)
As a future data scientist, I believe my primary professional responsibilities are to anticipate harm, challenge unjust systems, and ensure that my work does not amplify inequity. I prioritize fairness and accountability over efficiency or performance metrics. When professional obligations conflict, I choose the path that protects affected communities, even if it means slowing development, refusing deployment, or escalating concerns. I reject the idea that technical roles are neutral; data scientists are responsible for the downstream consequences of the systems they build, including those mediated through institutions and other agents.

### 3B. Analysis Using Professional Standards
My stance aligns strongly with the ACM Code of Ethics, particularly its emphasis on avoiding harm, ensuring fairness, and being accountable for computing systems’ impacts. It also aligns with the IEEE Code of Ethics, which stresses public welfare and transparency. However, I go further than these standards by explicitly prioritizing justice over organizational goals. Professional codes often frame ethics as a balance between competing interests; my position rejects that framing when vulnerable groups are at risk.

This course reinforced that data scientists cannot hide behind organizational structures. The Williams case showed how responsibility diffuses when no one claims ownership of harm. My professional stance is a direct response to that: I believe data scientists must actively resist responsibility gaps by documenting risks, challenging flawed assumptions, and refusing to participate in deployments that violate ethical principles.

My position also incorporates the course’s emphasis on mediation. Professional responsibility does not end at model performance; it extends to how systems reshape workflows, influence human judgment, and distribute authority. This broader view of responsibility is essential for ethical practice in real‑world environments.

### 4. Alignment, Tension, and Governmental Authority
My personal and professional ethical positions align in their prioritization of justice, transparency, and protection of vulnerable groups. Both emphasize responsibility, both reject neutrality, and both view harm as a systemic phenomenon. The main tension arises in scope: my personal ethics focus on moral commitments, while my professional ethics focus on obligations within institutional and technical contexts. In practice, this means my personal ethics sometimes demand stronger action than my professional role might formally require.

Governmental authority introduces another layer of tension. Regulations like the EU AI Act establish minimum standards, but my ethical positions often exceed those requirements. For example, the Act focuses on system classification and documentation, but my stance demands deeper scrutiny of institutional workflows and power dynamics. When regulations fall short, I believe data scientists have a duty to act ethically even when not legally required.

Another tension emerges when organizational goals conflict with ethical commitments. My personal ethics would push me to resist harmful deployments, while my professional role might pressure me to comply. In such cases, I believe my personal ethics must override institutional expectations. Ethical integrity requires the willingness to say no, even when doing so carries professional risk.

Ultimately, alignment between personal and professional ethics strengthens my ability to navigate these tensions. Both frameworks guide me toward the same principle: protect people first, especially those most vulnerable to systemic harm.

## 5. Position Resilience & Future Scenario
### 5A. Future Scenario (Plausible, Not Sci‑Fi)
In 2034, major cities adopt “predictive mobility systems” that allocate public transportation resources dynamically using real‑time data from phones, sensors, and travel histories. The system optimizes routes based on predicted demand, but it also deprioritizes neighborhoods with historically lower ridership — many of which are low‑income or marginalized communities. As a data scientist on the city’s analytics team, I am asked to improve the model’s efficiency by further weighting high‑demand zones.

### 5B. Stress Test of My Ethical Positions
This scenario challenges my values directly. Prioritizing efficiency would improve overall system performance but worsen inequity. My personal commitment to justice would push me to resist changes that harm underserved communities. Professionally, I would be obligated to raise concerns, document risks, and propose alternatives. However, institutional pressure to reduce costs might conflict with these commitments.

Transparency becomes difficult because the system’s optimization logic is complex and politically sensitive. Human dignity is at risk because entire communities could be treated as low‑value data points. This scenario tests whether I am willing to challenge institutional goals when the harm is indirect, systemic, and spread across time.

### 5C. Adaptation & Revision
This scenario reveals that my ethical positions need to incorporate procedural justice, not just distributive justice. It is not enough to protect vulnerable groups; I must also advocate for inclusive decision‑making processes that give affected communities a voice. I also need to strengthen my stance on algorithmic governance, recognizing that resisting harmful deployments may require coalition‑building, public communication, and political engagement.

My core values remain resilient, but the scenario shows that ethical practice requires more than principled stances — it requires strategic action within complex institutions. I would revise my professional position to explicitly include advocacy and participatory design as essential components of ethical data science.
