#Journal Entry – Week 4
Ethical Framework Analysis
##Part 1 — System Overview
System Chosen:
Automated Hiring Algorithm used by companies to screen job applicants

Brief Description
The system automatically evaluates résumés and application materials to determine which candidates advance to interviews. It may use keyword matching, machine‑learning models, or inferred traits to score applicants.

Stakeholders
Job applicants

Hiring managers and HR staff

The company using the system

Communities affected by employment opportunities

Regulators and labor rights organizations

##Part 2 — Ethical Framework 1 (Course Framework)
Framework Chosen: Utilitarianism
Core Principles
Actions are morally right if they maximize overall well‑being.

Harms and benefits must be evaluated across all affected stakeholders.

The morally correct action is the one that produces the greatest net good.

Why This Framework Applies
Utilitarianism is useful for evaluating large‑scale sociotechnical systems because it requires examining aggregate outcomes, not just individual cases. Automated hiring affects thousands of applicants, so utilitarian reasoning helps assess broad social impact.

##Part 3 — Ethical Framework 2 (Independent Framework)
Framework Chosen: Rawlsian Justice (Justice as Fairness)
Core Principles
Social and economic inequalities are acceptable only if they benefit the least advantaged.

Decisions should be evaluated from behind a veil of ignorance, where no one knows their own position in society.

Fairness, equal opportunity, and protection of vulnerable groups are central.

Why This Framework Applies
Hiring systems directly influence access to economic opportunity. Rawlsian justice provides a strong lens for evaluating whether the system treats all applicants fairly and whether it disadvantages already marginalized groups.

##Part 4 — Applying Both Frameworks to the System
###4.1 Utilitarian Analysis
Potential Benefits
Faster processing of applications

Reduced workload for HR staff

Lower hiring costs

Potentially more consistent evaluation than human reviewers

Faster matching of candidates to jobs

Potential Harms
Biased training data can produce discriminatory outcomes

Qualified candidates may be excluded

Lack of transparency reduces trust

Systemic inequality may be reinforced

Emotional and psychological harm to rejected applicants

Utilitarian Conclusion
If the algorithm reduces overall harm and improves total well‑being, it could be justified.
However, if it systematically disadvantages certain groups or reduces fairness, the net harm outweighs the benefits, making the system ethically unacceptable under utilitarianism.

###4.2 Rawlsian Justice Analysis
Key Questions
Would I accept this system if I didn’t know my race, gender, education, or socioeconomic status?

Does the system improve opportunities for the least advantaged?

Does it preserve fair equality of opportunity?

Rawlsian Evaluation
If the system disproportionately harms marginalized groups, it violates the difference principle.

If applicants cannot understand or challenge decisions, it violates fair equality of opportunity.

If the system amplifies historical bias, it fails the veil of ignorance test.

Rawlsian Conclusion
Under Rawlsian justice, the hiring algorithm is unethical unless it can be shown to:

reduce inequality,

improve opportunities for disadvantaged applicants, and

operate transparently and fairly.

##Part 5 — Comparing the Frameworks
Where They Agree
Both frameworks reject discrimination.

Both require evaluating the system’s real‑world outcomes, not just intentions.

Both emphasize the importance of fairness and minimizing harm.

Where They Differ
Utilitarianism focuses on total well‑being, even if some individuals are harmed.

Rawlsian justice prioritizes the least advantaged, even if total utility is lower.

Utilitarianism might accept the system if it benefits the majority.

Rawlsian justice rejects the system if it harms vulnerable groups, regardless of majority benefit.

Which Framework Is More Demanding?
Rawlsian justice is stricter because it requires fairness for all, not just overall benefit.

##Part 6 — Meta‑Evaluation
Which Framework Better Addresses This System?
Rawlsian Justice provides a stronger ethical evaluation for hiring algorithms because:

Hiring is fundamentally about opportunity, not efficiency.

Fairness and equal access are more important than aggregate utility.

The system’s potential to reinforce inequality is central to its ethical evaluation.

Rawlsian reasoning highlights harms that utilitarianism might overlook.

##Part 7 — Reflection
This assignment helped me understand that ethical evaluation is not just about identifying harms but about choosing the right moral lens. Utilitarianism highlights large‑scale consequences, while Rawlsian justice emphasizes fairness and structural inequality. Applying both frameworks revealed that automated hiring systems raise deep concerns about justice, transparency, and equal opportunity. Ethical analysis requires not only identifying harms but also understanding whose interests matter most and how systems shape society over time.
