## Section 1 - Reflection on Ethical Development

At the beginning of the semester, I thought of ethics as doing what is right in a specific situation while staying within what the law allows. My first way of judging right and wrong was personal. I asked whether I would accept the same situation if it happened to me. 

My thinking became more complicated when the course moved from individual choices to systems. I started with the belief that a machine can follow ethical instructions to a limited extent, but that the machine itself is not ethical. I still hold that view, at the end of the day, generative AI is just that, numbers that produce an output that would most likely please the user. AI systems can produce intelligent outputs and follow rules in some cases, but they do not carry moral responsibility the way people and institutions do.

My understanding of intelligence also became more precise. I see intelligence as what someone or something can understand based on knowledge, experience, observation, and willingness to learn. I do not think consciousness is required for intelligence. AI can be intelligent in the sense that it can process information, learn patterns, and generate useful outputs, but that does not mean AI is conscious or morally responsible.

The harm exercises, including the trolley problem, helped me think about cases where every option causes harm. My position became that when no option avoids harm, the better decision is the one that causes the least serious and least unfair harm. I also became more aware that harms can compete with one another. Harm can be physical, economic, emotional, social, institutional, or reputational. That means ethical judgment cannot depend on one simple measurement.

The AI hiring assignments helped me the most in understanding my viewing on the topic of ethics, given that I'm also very familiar with. In Week 5, I talked about an employer deciding whether to move an applicant forward or reject them using an AI hiring system.

## Section 2 - Personal Ethical Position Statement

### A. Personal Ethical Position Statement

I should act in a way I could defend if I were the person most affected by the decision. When values conflict, I always try to prioritize safety, privacy, fairness, especially in times like this where someone can find out everything about you in a matter of hours, along with reduced harm over convenience, speed, or profit, especially when a decision affects people with less power or less information. When all options are harmful, I support the choice that causes the least serious harm, creates the least unfair burden, and leaves room for correction or appeal. I do not treat machines as moral agents, even when they appear intelligent. People and institutions remain responsible for how their systems are designed, deployed, used, and corrected.

### B. Analysis and Influences

This position connects to several ideas from the course. My first influence is the idea that I should judge an action by asking whether I could accept being treated that way. This does not solve every ethical problem, but it prevents me from focusing only on my own convenience or the convenience of a company or institution. It pushes me to think from the perspective of the person who carries the burden of a decision.

My position also connects to consequentialism because I care about outcomes and harm reduction. At the same time, I do not think outcomes are the only thing that matters. A harmful action is not always acceptable just because it creates efficiency for a larger group. This is where my view connects to deontological thinking. Some duties, such as respecting privacy, avoiding deception, and not treating people only as data points, matter even when breaking those duties would be convenient.

The capability approach also shaped my position. In the AI hiring assignment, I argued that people need a real opportunity to compete, not only a formal opportunity. A process can look equal and still be unfair if some people face hidden barriers. That idea applies beyond hiring. If a system limits a person’s ability to understand, respond, appeal, or participate, then harm exists even if the system looks efficient.

## Section 3 - Professional Ethical Position Statement

### A. Professional Ethical Position Statement

As a future data scientist, my professional ethical position is that I should provide useful and accurate service while protecting people’s safety, privacy, and dignity, especially when sensitive data is involved. I should collect only the data needed for a clear purpose, explain limitations honestly, test for bias and failure, and avoid presenting uncertain outputs as certain decisions. When privacy, fairness, and efficiency conflict, I prioritize privacy and safety first, then fairness and accountability, then efficiency. I should not deploy or support a system if I know that affected people cannot understand, challenge, or recover from serious errors. Professional responsibility cannot be delegated fully to a model, a vendor, or an organization.

### B. Analysis: Professional Standards and Influences

This professional position aligns with the ACM Code of Ethics. The ACM Code says computing professionals should support the public good, avoid harm, be honest and trustworthy, respect privacy, and ensure fairness. That supports my view that technical success is not enough. A system can work in a narrow technical sense and still fail ethically if it exposes people to preventable harm, hides important limitations, or shifts risk onto users.

My position also aligns with the IEEE Code of Ethics. IEEE emphasizes safety, health, welfare, responsible behavior, ethical design, privacy, honest claims, and fair treatment. These expectations matter for data science because data systems can shape access to jobs, education, health care, money, and public information. A data scientist should not treat a model as neutral just because it uses numbers. Data reflects choices about what to collect, what to ignore, and what counts as success.

The European Commission’s Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI also support this view. The guidelines describe trustworthy AI as lawful, ethical, and robust. That matters because a professional data scientist must think beyond model performance. A system should be lawful, but it should also respect human agency, transparency, accountability, and robustness.

There is also tension between professional duty and organizational pressure. A company may want faster deployment, more data collection, or less explanation because those choices reduce cost. My professional position says that a data scientist should push back when those choices create avoidable risk. The system should always have a clear purpose, documented assumptions, tested limits, privacy protection, human review where needed, and a way for affected people to correct errors.


## Section 4 - Alignment, Tension, and Governmental Authority

My personal and professional positions align in their main commitments. Both prioritize safety, privacy, fairness, and reduced harm. Both reject the idea that a machine can carry moral responsibility by itself. Both also treat law as important but incomplete. Legal permission can create a boundary for action, but it does not always settle whether an action is right.

The main difference is that my personal position starts with empathy and moral self-checking, while my professional position adds technical duties. Personally, I ask whether I could accept being treated the same way. Professionally, I must also ask whether the data is necessary, whether the model is tested, whether affected people can understand the process, and whether the system has accountability. The professional role requires more documentation, evidence, and process.

A conflict could happen if I feel that a system feels unfair, but my professional role requires me to work inside an organization that has already approved it. In that case, I do not think the right response is to ignore the concern. I should document the issue, raise it through proper channels, recommend safeguards, and refuse to support uses that create serious and preventable harm.

At the same time, regulation cannot replace ethical judgment. Laws can be delayed, narrow, outdated, or shaped by political compromise and interest. (Facebook lobbying for age verifications in the US to collect data) Some harmful systems may not be covered clearly. Some legal systems may also permit practices that are still ethically weak. For that reason, professional responsibility remains even when regulation is absent or incomplete. Government authority should support ethics, but it should not become the only source of ethical responsibility.

## Section 5 - Position Resilience and Future Capabilities

### A. Future Scenario

Another future scenario is a university that uses an integrated AI student-support system. The system combines learning management data, attendance records, advising notes, financial aid information, campus access logs, and optional mental health check-in data. It predicts which students are at risk of failing, dropping out, or needing urgent support. Advisors receive ranked lists and suggested interventions. In some cases, the system automatically sends notifications, blocks course registration until a meeting occurs, or alerts campus support staff.

This system could help students who might otherwise be missed. It could identify academic risk early and connect students with resources. It could also create serious ethical pressure. The same system that helps one student could invade another student’s privacy, misclassify someone because of incomplete data, or create a permanent risk label that follows them through the institution.

### B. Stress Test of My Positions

My personal position remains stable in one important way. I would still ask whether I could accept being the student affected by the system. If I were struggling, I might want help before the situation got worse. But I would also want to know what data was being used, who could see it, and whether I could challenge a wrong label. This makes the scenario difficult because help and surveillance can look similar from the outside.

My professional position also holds, but it becomes harder to apply. Privacy and safety may conflict. More data may improve prediction, but it also increases privacy risk. A model may be more accurate if it uses sensitive data, but using that data can expose students to stigma or institutional control. Transparency can also conflict with harm prevention. If the system reveals every risk rule, some students or departments may game the system, though if the system hides too much, students lose observability and control.

### C. Adaptation and Revision

This stress test shows that my position should be refined with a stronger rule about proportionality. Sensitive data should only be used when the expected benefit is serious, specific, and not reasonably possible through less invasive means. The system should not collect data just because it might be useful later.

I need to specify that people affected by predictive systems need meaningful notice and contestability. In the university scenario, students should know that the system exists, what broad categories of data it uses, who can see the outputs, and how to request correction or review. The system should not automatically punish students based on a risk score. Human review should be required before any serious restriction, such as blocking registration or escalating a concern.

My positions can adapt to future technological change if I keep them focused on people, harm, and responsibility instead of specific tools. Technologies will change, data sources will expand, AI systems may become more autonomous, we cannot prevent that. The core ethical questions still matter: who is affected, who can see the decision pathway, who can challenge it, who controls it, and who is responsible when it fails. I am confident in those questions, but I would keep revising my position as systems become more complex.
