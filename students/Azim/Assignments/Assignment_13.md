## Section 1 – Reflection on Ethical Development

At the beginning of this course, I thought about ethics mostly as choosing the “right” action. I also thought about AI systems more like tools: a person builds them, a person uses them, and then that person is responsible for the result. I still think individual responsibility matters, but now I understand that ethical problems in data science are usually not that simple. A decision can be spread across many people, datasets, rules, platforms, models, dashboards, and institutions. Because of that, responsibility can become unclear even when harm is real.

The early assignments helped me see this. The Moral Machine work made me think about the difference between what many people choose and what is actually ethical. Crowd responses can show patterns, but they cannot automatically decide what is morally right. The intelligence assignment also made me think more about agency. A system can appear intelligent because it predicts, ranks, recommends, or generates outputs, but that does not mean it understands responsibility. This matters because people often treat AI outputs as neutral or objective, even when they are produced through human choices, data limits, and system design.

The harm and ethical framework assignments made ethics feel more practical. Harm is not only physical. It can also be economic, emotional, reputational, social, or systemic. An automated hiring, grading, credit, or moderation system may not physically injure someone, but it can still damage their opportunities, dignity, trust, or ability to participate in society. I also learned that different ethical frameworks notice different things. Consequentialism focuses on outcomes, deontology focuses on duties and rights, and virtue ethics focuses on character and judgment. I do not think one framework solves every problem, but comparing frameworks makes the analysis stronger.

The biggest change in my thinking came from BBS and mediation pathways. Before this course, I would usually ask, “Who made the decision?” Now I also ask, “Where did the information come from, how was it transformed, who had control, who could see what was happening, and who was affected at the end?” This matters because many AI decisions are not made in one clean place. They may come from user data, training data, model outputs, company policy, interface design, manager interpretation, and regulation at the same time.

The COVID-19 misinformation moderation case made this clear. The case is not only about whether false health information should be removed. It is also about who decides what is false, how platforms apply rules at scale, how governments influence private platforms, and what users can understand or appeal. Platforms may have operational control, governments may have public authority, health experts may have knowledge, and users may carry the consequences. By the end of the course, my view became more cautious but also more structured. I still believe technology can help people, but I do not think efficiency, innovation, or technical performance are enough by themselves. A system should be judged by how it affects real people, especially people who have less power to understand, challenge, or escape it.

## Section 2 – Personal Ethical Position Statement

### A. Personal Ethical Position Statement

My personal ethical position is that human dignity and well-being should come before efficiency, profit, or technical possibility. A decision is more ethical when it respects people as more than data points, gives them a fair chance to understand what is happening, and avoids serious harm that they cannot challenge. When values conflict, I would prioritize preventing serious harm and protecting basic rights over speed, convenience, or optimization. I also believe uncertainty should make us more careful, not less responsible. If I do not fully understand how a system affects people, I should not pretend the risk is not there.

### B. Analysis and Influences

This position connects to several ideas from the course. It connects to consequentialism because I care about outcomes and harms. If a system improves efficiency but creates unfair harm for a group of people, the efficiency is not enough to justify it. It also connects to deontology because I think some duties should not disappear just because ignoring them is convenient. People should not be manipulated, secretly surveilled, or denied important opportunities without explanation just because a system works faster that way.

I also see a connection to virtue ethics. A good professional should not only follow rules after being forced to. They should try to be careful, honest, and responsible before harm happens. In data science, this matters because many harms are not obvious immediately. A model may look accurate overall but still fail specific groups. A dashboard may look useful for managers but create fear for workers. A moderation system may reduce harmful posts but also silence legitimate speech or uncertainty.

The COVID-19 misinformation case influenced me because it showed that ethical choices can be uncomfortable in both directions. Removing misinformation can protect public health, but over-removal can harm expression, trust, and transparency. Doing nothing can protect speech, but it can also allow harmful false claims to spread. This is why I do not think ethics is just choosing a side. It is building a process that can handle tradeoffs honestly.

BBS also shaped my position. A person may send a message, an algorithm may rank it, a platform may label it, a government may pressure the platform, and a user may see only the final result. In that chain, meaning and responsibility move through different sources, vectors, and destinations. My view is that the more mediated and powerful a system becomes, the more responsibility designers and institutions have to make the process visible and contestable.

## Section 3 – Professional Ethical Position Statement

### A. Professional Ethical Position Statement

As a future data science professional, my ethical position is that I should help design, evaluate, and deploy data systems only when they include reasonable protection against unfair harm, misuse, and hidden decision-making. I would commit to explaining meaningful AI or data use when it affects people, checking systems for bias and foreseeable misuse before release, and supporting human review and appeal in high-stakes contexts. If company goals, deadlines, or professional pressure conflict with these duties, I would treat safety, fairness, privacy, and accountability as more important than simply shipping the system faster.

### B. Analysis: Professional Standards and Influences

This professional position connects to the ACM and IEEE style of professional responsibility discussed in class. I understand these codes as saying that technical professionals do not only owe loyalty to an employer or project. They also have duties toward the public, users, and people affected by the system. This matters because a data scientist can cause harm without directly seeing the affected person. If my work helps build a model, ranking system, dashboard, or moderation tool, I still share some responsibility for how that system is used.

One professional commitment I would make is transparency, but not as a vague word. I mean that people should be told when AI meaningfully affects a decision about them, especially in important areas like hiring, education, finance, healthcare, policing, or public services. Not every technical detail has to be explained to every user. But users should understand the basic role of the system, what kind of data matters, what the consequences are, and how they can challenge a wrong result.

Another commitment is bias and harm testing. I do not think it is enough to say that a model is accurate overall. A professional should ask who the model works for, who it fails, and what happens when it fails. A small error in a recommendation system is not the same as an error in medical triage, hiring, credit scoring, or content moderation during a public-health crisis. Context changes the ethical weight of the error.

My professional boundary is that I would not build, support, deploy, or approve a system that makes high-stakes decisions about people without meaningful human review, documentation, and an appeal process. I would also not support systems where the purpose is manipulation, hidden surveillance, or avoiding accountability. If a system cannot explain its basic decision pathway, cannot be audited, or gives affected people no way to contest serious outcomes, I would see that as a major ethical problem.

I understand that professionals work inside organizations and may not control everything. A junior data scientist may not decide the whole policy. But that does not remove responsibility completely. A professional can document concerns, ask for testing, request clearer communication, escalate risks, or refuse to present an uncertain system as reliable. Ethical professionalism means doing what is possible from your role and not hiding behind the phrase “the system decided.”

## Section 4 – Alignment, Tension, and Governmental Authority

My personal and professional positions mostly align. Personally, I believe dignity and harm prevention should come before efficiency. Professionally, I apply the same idea through transparency, bias testing, human review, and appeal. In both cases, my main concern is that people should not be treated like objects inside a technical process they cannot understand or challenge.

The tension is that professional life has constraints that personal ethics does not always have. In a company, there may be deadlines, budgets, legal minimums, and pressure from managers or clients. A system may not be perfect, but the organization may still want to launch it. My personal view might say, “Do not release it until the risks are solved.” My professional role may require a more practical approach: identify the biggest risks, create mitigation steps, document limitations, and argue for safeguards. The professional version of my ethics needs to be realistic, but it should not give up the core principle.

Government regulation is important, but I do not think law and ethics are the same thing. A law can set minimum rules, but a system can be legal and still ethically weak. The EU AI Act is useful because it tries to classify AI systems by risk and connect those risks to obligations like transparency, documentation, oversight, and risk management. That is a good direction because it forces organizations to think about AI systems as systems that can affect rights, opportunities, and public trust.

However, regulation can leave gaps. Technology changes quickly, and laws are often slower. A system may fall outside a strict legal category but still cause harm in practice. The COVID misinformation case shows this because authority, control, and responsibility are spread across public-health institutions, platforms, fact-checkers, automated systems, regulators, and users. A government may have public authority, but the platform may have operational control. A user may be affected by a moderation decision but may not know whether it came from a rule, an algorithm, a government request, or a human reviewer.

For me, legal compliance should be the floor, not the ceiling. Ethical responsibility should also include asking whether affected people have visibility, whether harms are distributed unfairly, whether there is an appeal process, and whether the system can be audited.

## Section 5 – Position Resilience and Future Capabilities

### A. Future Scenario

A plausible future scenario is an AI moderation and public-information system used during a future health emergency. Compared with COVID-19, the system is more advanced. It can detect misinformation across social media, private groups, video, audio, images, and AI-generated content in many languages. It can automatically label, downrank, remove, or redirect content. It also gives public-health agencies a dashboard showing misinformation trends and suggested interventions.

The goal is good: reduce harmful false information and help people find reliable health guidance. But the system is difficult ethically because future AI-generated misinformation may look very real, spread very fast, and change constantly. At the same time, scientific knowledge during an emergency may also change. A claim that looks wrong in the first week may become uncertain later. The system could protect people, but it could also over-control speech, hide uncertainty, or make public trust worse.

### B. Stress Test of My Positions

My personal position remains mostly stable in this scenario. I would still say that human dignity and well-being matter more than efficiency. I would also still say that uncertainty should increase responsibility. If a system can suppress speech or influence public understanding at large scale, it should be handled carefully.

My professional position also remains stable in some ways. I would still require transparency, bias testing, human oversight, and appeal. I would still not support a system that restricts important content with no explanation. I would also want documentation of who has authority, who has control, and who can review the system’s decisions.

But the scenario makes my position harder to apply. In an emergency, speed matters. Waiting too long to moderate harmful misinformation could lead to physical harm. But acting too quickly could silence people unfairly or damage trust. My position says human review is important, but in a fast-moving crisis there may be too much content for full human review. My position says transparency is important, but full transparency could also help bad actors avoid detection.

This is the unresolved tension: the same safeguards that protect people can sometimes slow down protective action or create new risks. So my framework cannot just say “always disclose everything” or “always require full human review.” It needs to be more specific.

### C. Adaptation and Revision

After stress-testing my position, I would revise it by saying safeguards should be proportionate to the risk and time pressure, but never completely removed. In a health emergency, some temporary automated actions may be justified, but they should have time limits, documentation, later review, and appeal options. The system should also distinguish between different types of content. Obvious scams or dangerous false medical instructions are not the same as uncertain scientific debate.

I would also clarify that transparency does not always mean revealing every technical detail. It means giving affected people and oversight bodies enough information to understand the system’s purpose, rules, authority structure, and ways to challenge mistakes. Some details may need to be protected from abuse, but that should not become an excuse for total secrecy.

Overall, I think my ethical position is fairly strong, but it needs to stay flexible. The main principle should remain stable: people should not be harmed, manipulated, or controlled by systems they cannot understand or challenge. The exact safeguards may change depending on the system, the risk, and the social context.
